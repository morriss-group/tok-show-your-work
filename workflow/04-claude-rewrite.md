I'll take the yes, and I'll take it with a number on it.

I repair appliances for a living, and I build AI systems on the side. Every piece of research my AI does gets checked by a second, independent AI before I act on it, because I've caught it wrong three times in one week on things that would have cost me money. So I come at this question as somebody who has watched a confident, fluent, well-written answer be flat wrong.

Clifford's shipowner is the whole argument for me. He didn't lie. He believed his ship was sound. Clifford says he still had no right to that belief on the evidence he had, and if the ship had made it across, he wouldn't have been innocent, just not found out. Now put that man on a phone. He doesn't have to believe the rumor anymore. He just has to forward it. In India in 2018, forwarded rumors about child kidnappers got about two dozen innocent people killed by mobs. Nobody who hit "forward" believed they were pulling a trigger. They were the shipowner with a light heart.

So yes, there's a responsibility, and it scales with two things: how many people you can reach, and what happens to somebody if you're wrong. Five friends in a group chat is one duty. A hundred thousand strangers is another. A joke is one thing. A named person accused of a crime is another.

Two honest limits. First, verifying is expensive, and the people told to "do your own research" are usually the ones with the least time and tools to do it. A duty only the comfortable can afford isn't ethics, it's a membership fee. Second, "verified" can't just mean "the official checker agreed." The checkers have been wrong, and when they're wrong, the rule that stops a lynching is the same rule that buries a true story.

And the new version of the shipowner isn't stifling doubts about planks. He's asking a chatbot if something's true, getting a nice paragraph back, and hitting share. The doubt got outsourced and deleted. Asking a machine is not checking. Asking two machines that read the same internet is not checking twice.

Verify before you share, in proportion to how far it'll travel and how much it can hurt. That's not Clifford's "always and everywhere." It's a working rule, and it's the one I run my own shop on.
