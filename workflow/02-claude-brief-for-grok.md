RESEARCH BRIEF — IB Theory of Knowledge, Knowledge Question of the Week

QUESTION: "As knowers, do we have a moral responsibility to verify
information before sharing it in digital spaces?"

I need material for a class discussion contribution. Not an essay — a
well-sourced, balanced position with the strongest arguments on BOTH sides.
Cite real sources; flag where you're uncertain.

1. THE PHILOSOPHICAL FOUNDATION
   - W.K. Clifford, "The Ethics of Belief" (1877) — the shipowner parable
     and "it is wrong always, everywhere, and for anyone, to believe
     anything upon insufficient evidence." Explain his actual argument.
   - William James, "The Will to Believe" — the direct rebuttal. Why does
     James think Clifford is wrong, and does that rebuttal survive contact
     with digital-era sharing?
   - Social epistemology on TESTIMONY: when am I entitled to believe (and
     pass on) what someone else told me? Contrast reductionism vs.
     anti-reductionism about testimony.
   - Miranda Fricker on epistemic injustice — does a verification duty fall
     unequally on people with less access to verification resources?
   - Any current work specifically on the ethics of SHARING as distinct from
     the ethics of BELIEVING. Sharing is a speech act, not just a belief
     state — who has written on that distinction?

2. THE STRONGEST CASE FOR "YES"
   Build the best version, including: sharing confers implied endorsement;
   scale and irreversibility of digital amplification; harm cases where
   unverified sharing killed people (e.g. vaccine misinformation, lynchings
   triggered by WhatsApp rumors in India, 2018 — verify these examples).

3. THE STRONGEST CASE FOR "NO" (or "not always")
   Steelman it properly — a TOK class will punish a one-sided answer:
   - Verification is expensive; a universal duty is unmeetable and would
     silence people with less time, education, or access.
   - Some sharing IS the verification ("does anyone know if this is true?").
   - Who verifies the verifiers? Fact-checkers have their own biases and
     institutional interests.
   - A strong duty may privilege official/institutional sources and suppress
     legitimate dissent — historically, suppressed claims have sometimes
     been true.
   - Does the duty apply equally to a private group chat and a public post
     to 100,000 people? If not, the duty is contextual, not absolute.

4. THE DISTINCTIONS THAT DO REAL WORK
   Where exactly does responsibility change, and why:
   - Reach (5 friends vs. viral) · Framing ("this is true" vs. "is this
     true?") · Stakes (celebrity gossip vs. medical advice) · Expertise
     asymmetry · Whether the sharer profits

5. TOK-SPECIFIC ANGLE
   - Which Areas of Knowledge does this cut across?
   - Which Ways of Knowing are most at risk in digital sharing?
   - What second-order knowledge questions does this open (e.g. "how do we
     know when we have verified enough?"). What counts as "sufficient"
     verification, and who decides?

6. THE MACHINE PROBLEM (the part most answers will miss)
   AI now sits between knower and information. If someone asks an AI and
   shares its answer, has verification occurred? Cover: documented cases of
   AI models confidently asserting false things; whether "an AI told me"
   is a form of testimony; and whether cross-checking one AI against
   another constitutes verification or just doubles the confidence.

OUTPUT: a structured brief with sources, both sides argued honestly, and a
short list of the sharpest questions a student could raise in discussion.
Mark clearly anything you could not verify.
