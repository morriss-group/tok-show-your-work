Yes. The duty scales with how far the claim can travel and how much it can hurt someone if it is wrong.

Here's my rationale. I repair appliances for a living and build AI systems on the side, and every piece of research my AI does gets checked by a second model from a different company before I act on it, because I caught it wrong three times in one week on things that would have cost me money. So I come at this as somebody who has watched a confident, fluent, well-written answer be flat wrong.

There's an old story from an 1877 essay by W. K. Clifford that is the whole argument for me. A shipowner is about to send a worn-out ship full of families to sea. He has doubts. Instead of inspecting it, he talks himself out of the doubts, decides the ship is sound, and watches it sail with a light heart. It sinks. Clifford's point isn't just that he got people killed. It's that he had no right to that belief on the evidence he had, and if the ship had made it across, he wouldn't have been innocent, just not found out. Now put that man on a phone. He doesn't even have to believe the rumor anymore. He just has to forward it. In India in 2018, child-kidnapping rumors passed along on WhatsApp and similar apps got about two dozen innocent people killed by mobs. Almost none of the people who hit "forward" thought of themselves as pulling a trigger. They were the shipowner with a light heart.

So yes, there's a responsibility, and it scales with two things: how many people you can reach, and what happens to somebody if you're wrong. Five friends in a group chat is one duty. A hundred thousand strangers is another. A joke is one thing. A named person accused of a crime is another.

Two honest limits. First, verifying is expensive, and the people told to "do your own research" are usually the ones with the least time and tools to do it. A duty only the comfortable can afford isn't ethics, it's a membership fee. I felt that one directly: the research behind this comment came out of paid AI tools most people in this thread don't have, and the free version of the same question gives a thinner answer. Second, "verified" can't just mean "the official checker agreed." The checkers have been wrong, and when they're wrong, the rule that stops a lynching is the same rule that buries a true story.

And the new version of the shipowner isn't stifling doubts about planks. He's asking a chatbot whether something's true, getting a nice paragraph back, and hitting share. The doubt got outsourced and then deleted. Asking a machine is not checking. Asking two machines that read the same internet is not checking twice.

Verify before you share, in proportion to how far it'll travel and how much it can hurt. That's not "always and everywhere." It's a working rule, and it's the one I run my own shop on.
