# A note on the date: why this was possible in September 2026 and not a year earlier

This is a claim about tools, from a user, not a study. Dated, so it can be judged later.

## What I actually did this week

I typed a question into one AI, asked it to write a research assignment for a second AI from a different company, let the second one work for a while on its own, and got back a sourced briefing with philosophers, dated cases, and a section grading its own confidence. Then the first AI cut that into a comment in my voice, and a third, free tool answered the same question in one shot as a comparison. Separately, a computer in my house that used to mine cryptocurrency now runs a small open model that reads and summarizes hours of video captions every night, and the tool that runs my business phone line was tuned tonight by measuring the delay on 248 real calls. None of that required me to write code. I don't write code.

## What was different a year ago, as I understood it then

A year ago, in the fall of 2025, the common experience was one box: you typed a question, you got an answer, and you carried it to the next tool by hand. The answers already sounded confident. The thing that has changed most is not the confidence; it's that the tools now **run work over time** instead of answering once. The research assistant that produced file 03 planned, searched, checked, and wrote for a stretch on its own. The tool that manages my files reads them, edits them, runs programs, and reports back. Models small enough to run on a used graphics card became good enough to summarize a two-hour podcast usefully. And the cost of the "thinking" part dropped enough that re-reading a long document on every turn is a budget line, not a wall.

I'm careful with this. "A year ago you couldn't do this" is the kind of sentence Clifford would ask me to prove, and I can't prove the negative. What I can say is what I couldn't do a year ago: I couldn't hand one model's output to another model as an assignment, walk away, and come back to sources. I couldn't run any of it at home for free. And I didn't know what a repository was.

## What has not changed

- The models still make things up, fluently, including citations. The cases are in file 03, section 6, and they're from 2023 through 2026. The tools got faster and more capable at doing work; the underlying failure didn't go away.
- Two models agreeing is still not verification. Faster agreement is just faster.
- The reading is still the reading. A machine drafting my summary of Clifford didn't put Clifford in my head. Whether it's in my head is stated in `HUMAN.md`, not assumed.

## Why this belongs in a TOK folder

The question was whether knowers have a duty to verify before sharing. The honest addendum is that the *means* of verifying, and of producing something worth verifying, changed hands this year. The cost of a decent-looking answer fell to nearly nothing. The cost of a checked answer did not fall nearly as far. The gap between those two is where the duty now lives, and it's wider than it was.
