# To the students: why I showed the machine, and what I think is coming

This page is not part of the answer to the Knowledge Question. It's the reason I bothered to show the work behind it. If your teacher wants to keep the thread on Clifford, skip this. If you're wondering why a stranger who fixes appliances put his homework on the internet, read on.

## What I think you're being told

Somewhere between the phone policy and the plagiarism policy, a lot of you are hearing a version of "AI is cheating, don't use it." I understand why a school says that. A tool that writes your essay in nine seconds is a problem for anybody whose job is to find out whether you can write an essay.

Let me tell you what research looked like when I was where you are. I was in high school in 1996. If I had a question like this one, the tools were the Encyclopaedia Britannica on a shelf, if your family had a set, and the public library, where you found a book by flipping through a card catalog organized by the Dewey Decimal System and walking to the number on the spine. If the book wasn't there, the answer wasn't there. A question like "do we have a moral duty to verify before we share" would have taken me a Saturday to research badly. This week I researched it in an evening with two AI systems briefing each other, and the hard part wasn't finding Clifford. It was deciding whether to trust what came back. The finding got cheap. The trusting didn't.

But I think it's setting you up badly, and I'll tell you why in plain terms. I'm not a teacher and I'm not a technologist by training. I fix refrigerators and dryers. Three weeks ago I didn't know what GitHub was. Today I run my business phone line on an AI receptionist that books real appointments, I have a computer in my house that reads hours of video every night and tells me what mattered, and the research behind the comment you're reading was done by two AI systems working from a brief a third one wrote, and then checked, because they get things wrong. I do this on the edge of what these tools can do right now, as a customer, not a programmer. If a repairman can do that in 2026, the thing you're being told not to touch is the thing your future employers will assume you already know.

## What I think happens next

Here's my honest read, and I'll mark where I'm guessing.

**The jobs you're planning for are being planned against the last twenty years.** The map most of you are using, college, then a job that looks like the jobs your parents' friends have, was drawn for a world where a person's value at work was doing the work: writing the memo, reading the contract, building the spreadsheet, answering the phone. My phone is answered by software now. The memo is drafted by software. That part is not a prediction; I watch it every day.

**Some people who know more than me say the change is faster than I'd guess.** A founder of one of the big AI labs said last week that for any job that can be done remotely, your "economic life expectancy" is about two years. I don't repeat that as fact. It's a claim, from a man selling a book, and the same week the head of the biggest AI company in the world said the opposite, that job losses have been *slower* than he expected. I put both in front of you on purpose. This is a TOK class. Two confident men, opposite claims, same month. Which one has the evidence and which one has the incentive is your homework, not mine.

**What I'm fairly sure of is narrower than either of them.** The cost of producing something that *looks* like competent work has fallen to almost nothing. The cost of producing work that is actually right, checked, and safe to act on has not fallen nearly as much. That gap is where the next decade's jobs live. The person who can tell the difference between the two, who can point a machine at a problem, read what comes back, and know which sentence is wrong, that person is worth more, not less. The person who can only produce the first kind is competing with a free tool.

## What that means for what you should do

Not "don't use it." Something harder.

1. **Use it on things you already understand**, so you can catch it. You learn what these tools are bad at by watching them fail at something you know. Start there, not with the essay you can't write yet.
2. **Learn to check it.** That's the whole Knowledge Question, and it turns out it's the whole job. The folder this page sits in shows one way: brief, draft, rewrite, compare against a free tool, list what you verified and what you didn't. It's not fancy. It's just honest.
3. **Keep the reading.** I'll say this against my own interest, because I built systems so I wouldn't have to do the reading. Having a machine summarize Clifford for me did not put Clifford in my head. If you let the tool do the understanding, you'll be exactly the worker it replaces.
4. **Own something the machine can't.** Relationships, judgment, a trade, a reputation for being right. The people I see doing best with these tools aren't the ones who use them most. They're the ones who already knew something and used the tools to get further with it.
5. **Ask what a claim would cost if it's wrong**, before you pass it on. That was Clifford's question in 1877. It's a better career skill than anything on my list.

## The honest part

I could be wrong about the timeline. The tech industry has been wrong about timelines for as long as it's existed, and there are serious people arguing right now that the money behind these tools is a bubble that breaks before the jobs do. If that happens, everything above still holds, just slower. The skill I'm describing, using the machine and then checking it, is the one that's useful in both futures.

What I'm not wrong about: the world you graduate into will not be the one your plan was drawn for. Nobody's is. The difference this time is that you can see it coming, and the tool that's changing it is sitting in your pocket being called contraband.

Use it. Check it. Show your work. That's all this folder is.
